Requirements Phase: Write the Doc Nobody Asked For

Before you test a single trekking pole listing, you need to know what PeakAndPack is actually supposed to do. Skip this and you're not testing the app, you're testing your own assumptions about it.

By RN ~5 min read Series: AI-Augmented QA

A confession heard on every team: “I'll just start testing and figure out requirements as I go.”

Here's how that ends. You find a bug. You report it. The developer says, “that's not a bug, that's intended.” You have no document to point to. You just lost the argument, and ten minutes of everyone's time.

A requirements document is the thing you point to instead of your memory. That's the whole pitch.

Functional vs. non-functional, fast

Skip the textbook fog. Here's the version that sticks:

Functional: what it does
A trekker can add a tent to their cart. A trekker can apply a discount code at checkout.
Non-functional: how well it does it
The cart updates in under a second. The search results load even if 500 people are searching at the same time.

Quick test: if a broken version of the app could still technically satisfy the requirement, it's non-functional. “The page loads fast” can be satisfied badly, like loading fast but showing the wrong price. “A trekker can add a tent to their cart” either happens or it doesn't, there's no halfway version of that.

Acceptance criteria: the argument-ender

Given, When, Then forces a condition you can actually check, instead of a vague feeling.

Applying a discount at checkout

Given a cart with a $100 total

When the trekker applies SAVE10

Then the total becomes $90, not $0

That example isn't random. BUG-009 in PeakAndPack is exactly this: SAVE10 currently takes 100% off instead of 10%. Without a written criterion, that's just “a discount happened.” With it, it's a provable spec violation.

This is the whole point. Acceptance criteria turn “that feels wrong” into “that fails this specific condition.” One gets fixed. The other gets debated in a meeting.

Constraints and assumptions

Constraints are things you can't change: PeakAndPack runs on Render's free tier, so it sleeps after 15 minutes idle, so the first request after a nap is slow on purpose, not because something broke. Assumptions are things you're choosing to believe until told otherwise: all prices in USD, one currency, English only.

Writing these down protects you from looking like you don't understand the product, and protects the product from someone “fixing” something that was never broken.

What PeakAndPack's doc should cover

Feature areaWhat to capture
ProductsListing, sorting, pricing rules, what counts as a valid trekking item
CartTotal calculation, quantity changes, source of truth for price
CheckoutDiscount rules, stock checks, what blocks an order
AuthRequired fields, login rules, what counts as a valid session
OrdersWho sees which orders, what an order record must contain

Your deliverable

One file: peakandpack-requirements.md.

  • Functional requirements per feature area
  • Non-functional requirements (performance, security, usability)
  • Acceptance criteria in Given, When, Then for the riskiest flows
  • Known constraints (free-tier hosting, single currency)
  • Stated assumptions, until told otherwise

Time: 1 to 2 hours. No AI vs. manual vs. automation split here, there's nothing to automate yet. You're deciding what “correct” even means. Everything from Phase 2 on checks the app against this document.
