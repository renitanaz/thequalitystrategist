Cleared the CCAR-F Exam

Aug 2026 · CCAR-F Revision Series

A short write-up on how I studied, what actually mattered on exam day, and where to find good material - written right after sitting the Claude Certified Architect, Foundations exam.

It's an architect exam, not a developer exam

The single most useful thing to internalize before you start studying: this exam is testing architectural judgment, not implementation recall. You are not going to be asked to write out a hook script character-for-character or recite a config file from memory. You're asked to reason about why one approach is more reliable than another, how a mechanism actually behaves under a specific failure mode, and - just as often - why not a tempting-sounding alternative actually holds up.

Every scenario question in the sample set follows this pattern: a system is already misbehaving in some specific, plausible way, and you have to pick the fix that addresses the actual root cause rather than one that sounds reasonable on the surface. Knowing the exact syntax of a flag or a config key rarely decides the answer. Knowing why that mechanism exists, and what it does and doesn't guarantee, almost always does.

If you take one thing from this post: stop optimizing for memorizing exact syntax. Optimize for being able to explain, out loud, why a given design choice is more reliable than the obvious-sounding alternative.

How I studied

Two things did most of the work:

  • Understand the concepts first, properly. Not "I've heard of this," but being able to explain the mechanism to someone else without notes - what it does, when it fires, what it can't do. This is exactly what these D1–D5 notes were built for.
  • Write mock questions constantly. Not just answer them - write your own, in the same scenario-based style as the official sample questions. Writing a plausible wrong answer forces you to understand exactly why it's wrong, which is a much harder and more useful exercise than just picking the right option from someone else's list.

The official sample questions are worth working through slowly rather than fast. Each one usually tests two or three things at once - a named anti-pattern, a specific mechanism, and a design trade-off - and the wrong answers are deliberately the "sounds reasonable" kind, not obviously silly ones.
