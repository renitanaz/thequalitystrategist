What makes CMS testing different

Everything else in this series assumes you understand one thing first: a CMS-driven website isn't one thing changing on one schedule, it's two things changing on two different schedules, by two different kinds of people, and most of what goes wrong lives in the gap between them.

Key takeaways
  • A CMS splits a website into two layers: code (developer-owned, deployed through review) and content (editor-owned, published with one click, no review).
  • Content ships with zero code review, zero deploy step, and zero automatic test run, by design, not by accident.
  • This series is organized by SDLC phase, Plan, Build, Pre-release, Post-release, because that's where each kind of risk actually surfaces, not by CMS feature.
  • Content-layer risk mostly lives in Pre-release and Post-release, exactly the two phases a code-only test plan is least likely to cover.

This isn't only a QA series. Everything from here on, SEO, accessibility, performance, security, forms, is written as testing strategy because that's the most rigorous way to teach it, but the checks themselves are things a developer, a content editor, or a founder shipping their own site needs just as much. If there's no dedicated tester on your team, you're the one who has to catch these before a reader, a search engine, or a screen reader does.

Most of the web isn't hand-coded

Around 70% of all websites run on a content management system rather than hand-built pages. WordPress alone accounts for roughly 42% of every website on the internet, about 60% of the CMS market. If you test websites at all, odds are you're already testing a CMS, whether or not anyone called it that.

That matters because a CMS changes what "the website" actually is. It's not one artifact that a developer builds and ships. It's two artifacts, built by different people on different schedules, that only combine into a page at the moment someone requests it.

Two layers, two schedules, two owners

The developerShips the code layer through review and a deploy pipeline.
The editorShips the content layer with one click. No review, no pipeline.
Why this is the single most important idea in this series

A test plan written only for the code layer schedules testing around deploys and pull requests, exactly the moments a CMS lets editors bypass.

An editor can publish a broken page, an unoptimized image, or an unvalidated form on a Tuesday afternoon with zero code involved and zero automatic test run. A testing strategy that only triggers on code changes leaves that entire content layer untested, every time, by default.

Every post after this one exists because of that gap.

Where the CMS-specific risk actually comes from

None of this means the code layer stops mattering, plugins, themes, and templates carry their own real risk. It means the risk surface is wider than a typical test plan assumes, because it spans both layers plus the places they interact.

LayerTypical riskWho introduces itWhen it ships
CodeA plugin update changes markup structure, a theme change breaks a template, a third-party script slows every pageDevelopers, or a plugin's own maintainersOn deploy, or silently on plugin auto-update
ContentMissing alt text, no meta description, a broken internal link, an image uploaded at full resolution, a form field left unvalidated by the template so bad input goes straight throughEditors, marketers, anyone with publish accessThe moment someone clicks Publish
Where they meetA template assumes content will always be short, a plugin exposes a field the content model didn't anticipate, an integration breaks because a field an editor renamed was referenced by exact name elsewhereNobody, deliberately, it's an interaction neither side tested for aloneWhenever the specific combination first occurs, often long after both pieces individually shipped fine

The SDLC map this series follows

Rather than organize this series by CMS feature, it's organized by where in a project's lifecycle each kind of testing actually has to happen to catch something before it matters. That's the map every later post slots into.

Post 17, the capstone, turns this diagram into a full checklist with every post's specific checks slotted into its phase. For now, the point is simpler: content-layer risk mostly lives in Pre-release and Post-release, the two phases a code-only test plan is least likely to cover on a recurring basis, because nothing about a content edit triggers a build.

This is why the series doesn't anchor to one CMS. The Plan and Build risks differ meaningfully between WordPress, a headless setup, and something in-house. The Pre-release risks, SEO, accessibility, performance, analytics, and the rest, are close to identical regardless of what generated the page. Structuring the series this way is what makes the strategy transferable instead of a product tutorial.

See the split for yourself

The fastest way to feel the content/code separation, rather than just read about it, is to watch a content change go live with no deploy step at all.

Try it

Open wordpress.org/playground and click through to launch a fresh WordPress instance, it runs entirely in your browser, no install, no account, live in seconds.

Open the default sample post from the dashboard, edit its content directly: add a paragraph, insert an image without filling in alt text, bold some text. Click Update, then view the post on the live site.

Notice what didn't happen: no code review, no build step, no deploy pipeline, no test suite ran. The missing alt text you just added is live, immediately, exactly as if it were correct. That gap is what the rest of this series tests for.

Your deliverable

A short note, two or three sentences, on one CMS-driven site you already know (a company site, a blog, anything), naming one thing that could go wrong purely at the content layer, something an editor could break without touching any code. Keep it, Post 2 builds directly on this kind of thinking.

Time: ~15 minutes, most of it the WordPress Playground walkthrough above.
