SEO testing

A CMS generates most of a page's SEO signals from content-model fields, automatically, on every page, using the same template. When one of those fields is empty or duplicated, the mistake doesn't ship on one page, it ships on every page built from that template, silently, until someone actually checks.

Key takeaways
  • SEO fields (title, meta description, canonical) usually come from the content model, so a missing or empty field is a template-level bug that repeats on every page of that type.
  • Check title uniqueness, meta description presence, canonical tags, structured data, and redirect handling, in that order of how often they actually break on a CMS.
  • Structured data makes a page eligible for rich results (star ratings, FAQ snippets); its absence isn't visibly broken, just invisibly worse.
  • A renamed URL slug without a redirect is one of the most common CMS-specific SEO regressions, and it's entirely avoidable.
  • robots.txt and sitemap.xml are two small files worth checking directly. A leftover staging setting or a stale entry in either one is easy to ship and easy to miss.

Where SEO fields actually come from

On a hand-coded page, a missing title tag is a one-off mistake in one file. On a CMS, the title tag is usually generated from a template, pulling a field, a fallback, or both, and that same logic runs for every single page of that content type.

Why this shows up as duplicate content, not just a missing field

Search engines treat pages with identical titles and descriptions as a signal of duplicate or low-value content, even when the body text is completely different. A single forgotten field in a content model can quietly flag dozens of genuinely unique pages as duplicates.

That's exactly what happened on the Grove & Bean demo site: the About and Contact pages share an identical title (SEO-2), and the single blog post reuses the Journal listing page's generic title instead of its own (SEO-4).

Structured data: invisible until it isn't

Structured data (usually JSON-LD embedded in the page) tells a search engine explicitly what a piece of content is: an article, a product, an FAQ, a recipe. Without it, the page still gets indexed and ranked normally, it just isn't eligible for the enhanced result formats that structured data unlocks.

Missing structured data doesn't fail loudly. Nothing breaks, no error shows, the page just never qualifies for a rich result it otherwise could have. That's exactly why it needs an explicit check instead of being caught by "does the page look right."

Social share preview: a present tag isn't proof it renders

Open Graph tags control how a page looks when it's shared on LinkedIn, Facebook, or in a chat app like Slack or Teams: the title, description, and image that show up in the preview card. Confirming the tags exist in the page source is not the same as confirming the preview actually renders correctly.

Why this needs its own check, not just a source-code read

The same "looks right versus is right" gap from Post 9 applies here. A malformed or missing OG image tag can produce a preview with no image, a broken thumbnail, or a generic fallback, and the only way to see that is to actually generate a preview, not just read the markup.

Free validators do this directly: LinkedIn Post Inspector and Meta's Sharing Debugger both fetch a URL and show the exact card that platform would render.

Grove & Bean's blog post has no Open Graph tags at all (SEO-4). Pasted into LinkedIn or Slack, it would show a bare link with no title, description, or image, exactly the kind of silent, unglamorous SEO loss that never trips an error but quietly costs click-through.

Redirects: the CMS-specific regression

Renaming a URL slug, changing /blog/old-title to /blog/new-title, is often a single field edit for an editor. Nothing about the CMS forces a redirect from the old URL to the new one to be created at the same time.

Why this is worth testing even though it's easy to overlook

Every external link, bookmark, and search-engine index entry pointing at the old URL now 404s. Search engines eventually notice and drop the page's accumulated ranking history, effectively resetting it.

Testing this is simple once you know to look for it: rename a slug in a staging environment, then check whether the old URL redirects or dies. Most CMS platforms don't do this automatically unless a plugin or explicit setting handles it.

For checking redirects directly, httpstatus.io is worth knowing: it checks a single URL or a bulk list, and shows the full redirect chain and response headers, not just a final status code. That one tool covers what several overlapping redirect-checkers out there each do separately.

The technical checks nobody remembers

Four small, unglamorous files and behaviors sit underneath everything else in this post. None of them are hard to check. All of them are easy to forget, especially right after a migration (Post 16) or a staging-to-production promotion (Post 15).

CheckWhat it catches
robots.txtA blanket Disallow: / left over from staging blocks search engines from the entire live site. Open the file directly and read it, don't assume it's fine because it exists.
sitemap.xmlTwo separate failure modes: pages that exist but aren't listed (never efficiently discovered), and URLs that are listed but no longer exist (crawlers hit a 404). Check both directions, not just that the file loads.
Broken internal linksEditors introduce these through ordinary publishing, a renamed page, a typo in a manually-typed link. A free crawler like Dead Link Checker catches these in bulk, including inside the sitemap itself.
FaviconMissing entirely, or still showing a default framework icon, easy to spot once you're looking at the browser tab instead of the page content.

Grove & Bean fails all four. Its robots.txt disallows the entire site (SEO-6), its sitemap.xml is missing the blog post and lists a page that no longer exists (SEO-7), and no favicon is defined anywhere (SEO-8).

Try it: audit the demo site

Try it

Open each of the five pages in the Grove & Bean demo site, and for each one, view source (or DevTools → Elements) and note the <title>, whether a <meta name="description"> exists, whether a <link rel="canonical"> exists, and whether a favicon is defined.

Then open demo-site/robots.txt directly and read it. Separately open demo-site/sitemap.xml and compare its five entries against the five real pages in the folder: note which real page is missing, and which listed URL doesn't correspond to an actual page.

Finally, open Chrome DevTools → Lighthouse on the blog post page specifically, run an SEO audit, and see which of everything you just found it catches automatically versus what you only found by reading the files directly. (A quicker alternative for the on-page fields: the SEO Meta in 1 Click extension surfaces title, description, and headings without opening view-source at all.)

Check your findings against demo-site/seeded-issues.md when you're done, specifically the SEO-1 through SEO-8 entries.

Your deliverable

A short table: each of the five demo pages, its title, whether it has a meta description, and whether it has a canonical tag, plus the specific robots.txt and sitemap.xml problems you found. Note which issues Lighthouse's SEO audit caught automatically and which it missed.

Time: ~25 minutes.
